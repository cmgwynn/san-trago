Golden hour at Pacific Pour

Screen 1, service. Authored at 1600 x 900 (16:9) and rendered at 2x for sharpness. Illustrated style with smooth scaling, not pixel art.

Screen 1b: the serve

About a second and a half after each serve: the glass slides to the guest, they react, coins hop into the jar, and any new guest-book fact pops up where the book is. A perfect serve gets the medallion; a sloppy one gets a smaller reaction.

Screen 2: the guest book

Opened from the glowing book on the lower shelf or the book button. It pauses service, so reading is never punished. The new fact is highlighted where it was learned.

Screen 3: last call

The same bar after closing, with night falling. Money and stories get equal space: new guest-book facts are celebrated here as well as in the book. Serif type keeps this text-heavy screen readable, unlike the pixel font in concept 01.

What this concept proposes

Original, code-drawn placeholder art. It borrows the feeling of Strange Antiquities (a warm, dim, symmetrical shop with a framed visitor, parchment cards, and brass UI) and copies none of its assets, characters, or layout details. It is a mood target for the creative lead, not final art. A real illustrator's hand would replace every shape here.

Scene, front to back

  • Bar mat (center): the work area. The glass is drawn large, front and center under the guest, mid-pour (Mode C) with the brass gauge beside it. A tutorial-only tip sits next to it; once you know the controls it disappears.
  • Speed rail (under the glass): every pour source in one row, labeled on the item, with number keys 1 to 4. An empty slot shows where upgrades go. The active source glows.
  • Order rail (bottom left): one parchment ticket per guest. Recipe blocks carry a letter (R, C, D) as well as a color, and the selected ticket ticks off what you've poured. The selected guest stands in the arch; the others sit in shadow at the ends of the bar.
  • Back bar (bottom right): dirty glasses, sink, and washer share one compact strip, so washing never competes with the guest for attention. A hired busser takes this strip over.
  • Counter: the clean-glass rack, tip jar, Churro, and the guest book. The book glows with a badge when you learn something; opening it (screen 2) pauses service.
  • Churro: the bar cat. Pet him once a shift; he naps on the counter and swishes his tail.
  • Keepsakes: as you get to know regulars, they leave gifts on the shelves (Maya's surfboard, with a tag). Dashed empty spots hint at more.
  • The arch: the guest stands framed against a golden-hour Pacific, with Point Loma, the pier, and palms in silhouette. The guest is the focal point, as in a portrait. A single speech card holds small talk only; the order lives on the ticket.
  • Back bar: dark cabinets of bottles, plants, and keepsakes, lit by two warm sconces and a string of bistro lights.

Readability rules

  • The scene is dim; everything you can act on has a brass plate and glows on hover. Decor gets neither and stays in shadow.
  • Each piece of information appears once: one fill gauge (by the glass), one guest-book button (on the counter), and each label sits on its object.
  • Selection uses brass corner brackets, never only a color change.
  • Speech is parchment with serif type. Game numbers sit on brass, so dialogue and state never look alike.
  • Smallest text is 15 px at 1600 x 900, which is 12 px on a Steam Deck (1280 x 800) and 18 px at 1080p. No pixel font, so the results, shop, and guest-book screens have no font problem.
  • Every station has a number key, so the game can be played from the keyboard, and later from a controller with a selection cursor.
  • Patience is four sand dollars on each ticket: an icon with a count, readable at a glance. The last one turns red.
  • Fixed zones: top bar (money, settings), stage (back bar, arch, waiting guests), bar top (glass rack, mat, tip jar, Churro, guest book), and lower bar (orders, speed rail, back bar, day dial). Nothing overlaps the arch or the guest's face.

Production implications (for review)

  • Who paints? The creative lead could paint guests and props, or we could commission portraits. This is the biggest cost question.
  • Separate layers: view (swappable by time and neighborhood), back bar, guest portrait, counter, stations, and UI.
  • Guests: one bust per guest, 3 or 4 expressions, blink and breathing tweens. No walk cycles.
  • The guest book's fact system is prototyped in Godot (scripts/systems/guest_book.gd, with guests in content/guests/). Facts unlock from visits, perfect serves, serving a drink, or small talk, and can chain. Writing the facts is the content cost.
  • A gray-box of this layout runs in Godot (scenes/layout_lab.tscn). Tests check that zones and controls never overlap the guest's face and that the layout fits from 1024 x 768 to ultrawide.

Questions for the creative lead

  • Concept 01, concept 02, or a blend (for example, painted portraits over pixel stations)?
  • Waiting guests now sit in shadow at the ends of the bar. Is that enough presence, or too busy?
  • Which keepsakes should each regular leave, and at what point in their story?
  • Should the guest book pause service (as proposed), or stay live so reading costs time?
  • How dark is "dark but cozy"? Is this golden hour warm enough, or should it be brighter?
  • Which guest facts are fun to unlock: jobs, secrets, favorite drinks, relationships between guests?
  • Do you want to paint, or should we budget for commissioned art?
